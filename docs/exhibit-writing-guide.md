# Exhibit Writing Guide — Experium Park

*Based on analysis of Spirited Garden (생각하는정원), Jeju, South Korea.*

---

## Core Philosophy

These signs don't inform. They invite.

Every exhibit at Spirited Garden treats the visitor not as a student receiving facts, but as a person who has paused in front of something alive. The writing assumes the visitor is already present — it just helps them go deeper.

**The underlying belief:** A thing in nature is never just a thing. It is time made visible. The job of exhibit text is to reveal that time, and to let the visitor find themselves in it.

For Experium Park, this means: every exhibit about a banyan tree, a monitor lizard, a Hussain Sagar lotus, or a Deccan rock formation is also — quietly — about patience, resilience, belonging, or letting go. The exhibit names the nature. The writing finds the meaning.

---

## The Structure (3-Act Pattern)

Every sign at Spirited Garden follows a consistent arc. No exceptions.

### Act 1 — The Sensory Arrival (1–3 sentences)
Open with what the visitor can see, smell, or feel *right now*. Not a definition. Not a fun fact. A present-tense image that anchors them to this specific moment.

> *"Golden fruits nestled among green leaves line the path, forming a gentle corridor of citrus trees."*  
> *"The tree has lived for a hundred years with an empty core."*  
> *"Dol Hareubang is carved from stone, yet its expression is always gentle."*

**Rule:** The first sentence must work even if the visitor reads nothing else. It should make them look at the exhibit differently.

### Act 2 — The Reveal (the bulk of the text)
This is where you teach — but not through facts. Through reframing.

Take the thing's most surprising or counterintuitive truth and unfold it slowly. Structure it as a series of short revelations, each one building on the last.

The pattern inside Act 2:
1. Name the surface assumption ("At first glance, it may seem rough rather than beautiful.")
2. Complicate it ("Yet the quince tree knows that its true value does not lie in how it looks.")
3. Explain the hidden logic through the thing itself ("The scent does not appear when the flowers bloom...")
4. Anchor the specific details (age, origin, method, history — real numbers, real names where possible)
5. Pivot from the thing to the principle ("A deep fragrance is never easily gained.")

**Rule:** Every factual detail must earn its place by serving the meaning. Age of the tree → reveals time. Country of origin of stones → reveals diversity becoming unity. Don't list facts. Use facts as evidence for a truth.

### Act 3 — The Echo (1–3 sentences)
Close with a line that leaves the visitor with something to carry. Not a lesson spelled out. A quiet restatement that turns the exhibit's story back on the visitor's own life.

> *"The Heritage Citrus Garden is not simply a landscape of Jeju, but a quiet record of how Jeju has lived through time."*  
> *"Only when decay has made space can life truly widen its ground."*  
> *"Watering, therefore, is not merely a technique of maintenance, but an attitude of forming a relationship."*

**Rule:** The last line should land like a door quietly closing — not a bang, a soft click. If it sounds like a life lesson, soften it. If it sounds like a caption, deepen it.

## Voice and Tone Rules

### The voice is: slow, sure, generous
It does not rush to its point. It trusts the reader to follow. It never condescends.

**Sentence rhythm:** Short declarative sentences alternate with longer ones. This creates a breathing quality — like someone speaking unhurriedly.

> *"It is large and rugged. At first glance, it may seem rough rather than beautiful."* [short / short]  
> *"Because its fruit cannot be eaten raw, it was once dismissed as a 'disgraced' fruit."* [longer]  
> *"Yet the quince tree knows that its true value does not lie in how it looks."* [medium]

### The voice is: never the teacher
The signs never say "you should" or "this teaches us." Instead, they let the thing speak.

> Say: *"The plum tree speaks without words: only when decay has made space can life truly widen its ground."*  
> Don't say: *"This plum tree teaches us about resilience and acceptance."*

### The voice is: honest about difficulty
Several signs acknowledge hardship, imperfection, hollowness, sacrifice. They don't paper over it. The Hollow Plum Tree is hollow. The Sacrifice Branch is cut. The Saemaul Memorial was built on land without water or roads.

**Rule:** If the thing you're writing about has a hard side — an animal that scavenges, a tree that's invasive, an ecosystem that's dying — don't hide it. Find the meaning in it.

---

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|----|-------|
| Open with a sensory image | Open with a classification ("The banyan tree, *Ficus benghalensis*, is...") |
| Use the exhibit's own qualities as metaphor | Import metaphors from outside ("Like a soldier standing guard...") |
| Name real details: ages, origins, methods | Use vague superlatives ("one of the most important trees in India") |
| Let the thing "speak" or "wish" or "know" | Personify clumsily ("This cheerful tree loves sunshine!") |
| End with a quiet human echo | End with a call to action ("Come back and visit again!") |
| Acknowledge paradox and complexity | Oversimplify into a moral ("This shows us nature is strong") |
| Use "yet," "but," "not... but..." constructions | Use "therefore" and "thus" — too formal, breaks the voice |
| Trust the visitor to follow a long thought | Break up every insight into bullet points |
| Write in present tense for the exhibit's current state | Write in past tense unless describing history |

## Adapting for Experium Park's Context

Spirited Garden's texts carry a Buddhist-influenced contemplative tradition. Experium's visitors are families and school groups in Hyderabad. The philosophy translates — the cultural register needs adjusting.

**What to keep:**
- The 3-act structure
- Present-tense sensory opening
- Reframing through counterintuitive truth
- Specific real details (names, ages, origins, local ecology)
- The quiet human echo at the end

**What to adjust:**
- The pace can be slightly faster — 250–350 words vs. Spirited Garden's ~400
- The echo can be warmer and less austere — Hyderabadi audiences respond to connection and belonging, not just solitude
- Ground exhibits in *local* specificity: Deccan Plateau ecology, Hussain Sagar, Telangana seasons, species that Hyderabadis already know by name

**Tone calibration:**
Spirited Garden: contemplative, slightly austere, meditative  
Experium Park: warm, curious, grounded — like a wise friend explaining something remarkable they just noticed

## Writing Template

```
[TITLE — the exhibit's name, simple and direct]

[SENSORY ARRIVAL — 1–2 sentences. What does the visitor see/smell/hear right now?]

[SURFACE ASSUMPTION — 1 sentence. What might a visitor assume or not notice?]

[REFRAME — 2–4 sentences. The counterintuitive truth. Use the thing's own qualities.]

[SPECIFIC DETAILS — 2–4 sentences. Real facts: age, origin, local context, 
Hyderabad/Telangana connection. Each detail should serve the meaning.]

[BRIDGE TO PRINCIPLE — 1–2 sentences. The thing's particular truth becomes 
a wider truth. Still grounded in the thing, not floating into abstraction.]

[ECHO — 1–2 sentences. Quiet close. The visitor carries something away.]
```

### Example Application (Neem Tree, Experium Park)

> **The Neem Tree**
>
> The smell reaches you before the tree does. It is sharp and bitter — not the scent of something welcoming, but of something that has already decided what it is.
>
> For centuries, neem was called the village pharmacy, the free medicine of the poor. Yet it asks nothing in return for what it gives. Its leaves cure, its bark protects, its oil repels. It grows where little else will — in cracked soil, through dry summers, in the narrow strips of land no one else wanted.
>
> This particular tree has stood here for [X] years. In that time, Hyderabad has grown around it — the city expanding, the air thickening — and the neem has kept filtering what passes through its leaves. Its bitterness is not hostility. It is the taste of something that protects by being exactly what it is.
>
> The neem does not become sweeter to be liked. It remains bitter, and in its bitterness, it heals.

## Checklist Before Publishing

- [ ] First sentence: sensory, present tense, no classification
- [ ] A counterintuitive truth is revealed, not stated
- [ ] At least one specific real detail (age, origin, local name, ecological fact)
- [ ] The exhibit's nature provides the metaphor — nothing imported from outside
- [ ] Last sentence can be read alone and still land
- [ ] No bullet points in the exhibit text itself
- [ ] Word count: 200–350 words
- [ ] Read aloud: does it breathe? Are there places you'd naturally pause?
